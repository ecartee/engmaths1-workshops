    Practice: Powers of Sine and Cosine.

    🔗

    3.

    Write \(\sin^2(\theta)\cos^2(\theta)\) as a constant plus multiples of \(\cos(k\theta)\text{,}\) in two different ways.
    🔗

    (a)

    Use \(\cos(\theta)=\tfrac{1}{2}\left(e^{j\theta}+e^{-j\theta}\right)\) and \(\sin(\theta)=\tfrac{1}{2j}\left(e^{j\theta}-e^{-j\theta}\right)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Use \(\sin(2\theta)=2\sin(\theta)\cos(\theta)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Evaluate: Cosine of a Complex Number.

    🔗

    4.

    A student says that \(\lvert\cos(z)\rvert\le 1\) for every complex number \(z\text{,}\) because the graph of cosine stays between \(-1\) and \(1\text{.}\)
    🔗

    (a)

    Evaluate \(\cos(j\ln(2))\) exactly.
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Do you agree with the student? Explain your answer.

    Create: Your Own Eigenvalues.

    🔗

    5.

    Work with a neighbour on this exercise. If you are working alone, find the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of your own matrix in part (c).
    🔗

    (a)

    Let \(A=\begin{bmatrix}a \amp b\\c \amp d\end{bmatrix}\text{.}\) Show that the characteristic equation \(\det(A-\lambda I)=0\) can be written as \(\lambda^2-(a+d)\lambda+(ad-bc)=0\) for any numbers \(a\text{,}\) \(b\text{,}\) \(c\) and \(d\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Choose two different whole numbers and keep them to yourself. Use part (a) to write down a \(2\times 2\) matrix with no zero entries whose eigenvalues are your two numbers.
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Swap matrices with a neighbour. Find the eigenvalues of their matrix, and an eigenvector for each eigenvalue. Then swap back and check each other’s eigenvectors.
    🔗

    Practice: Powers of a Matrix.

    🔗

    6.

    Let
    \begin{equation*} M=\begin{bmatrix}3 \amp 1 \amp 5\\1 \amp 3 \amp -2\\0 \amp 0 \amp 4\end{bmatrix},\quad \vec{v}=\colvec{1\\-1\\0}. \end{equation*}
    🔗

    (a)

    Show that \(\vec{v}\) is an eigenvector of \(M\text{,}\) and find its eigenvalue.
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Use part (a) to find \(M^2\vec{v}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Find \(M^{2026}\vec{v}\text{.}\) Explain your reasoning.
    🔗

    Challenge: The Largest Eigenvalue Wins.

    🔗

    7.

    The matrix \(A\) from Exercise 1 has eigenvectors \(\colvec{1\\1}\) with eigenvalue \(5\) and \(\colvec{1\\-2}\) with eigenvalue \(2\text{.}\) Let \(\vec{v}=\colvec{2\\-1}\text{.}\)
    🔗

    (a)

    Write \(\vec{v}\) as a sum of multiples of the two eigenvectors.
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Find a formula for \(A^m\vec{v}\text{,}\) and check it when \(m=1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Explain in your own words why \(A^m\vec{v}\) points almost along \(\colvec{1\\1}\) when \(m\) is large.
    🔗
    🔗
    Google’s original PageRank ranked web pages with this idea. Multiplying by one huge matrix again and again picks out the eigenvector with the largest eigenvalue.
